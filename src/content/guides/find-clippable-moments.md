## Choose the viewer before the moment

A moment can be interesting to an existing fan and confusing to someone encountering the creator for the first time. Decide who the clip is for before searching the footage.

Write a short audience description: a beginner learning a game, an existing podcast listener, or someone comparing a particular tool. This helps you recognize whether the moment supplies useful information or only relies on familiarity with the speaker.

## Make a candidate log

Watch the recording once for meaning. Write down timestamps when you notice a clear explanation, a surprising result, a demonstration, a disagreement resolved through detail, or a reaction whose setup is easy to explain.

Use a small table in a document or notebook:

| Timestamp          | Main idea               | Context needed                      | Ending                  |
| ------------------ | ----------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Illustrative 12:40 | Why the dough collapsed | Show the dough and name the mistake | The corrected technique |
| Illustrative 28:10 | Unexpected game result  | Identify the challenge              | The player's reaction   |

These are examples of a logging format, not timestamps in a real recording. The log prevents you from repeatedly scrubbing through the same footage without making a decision.

## Check completeness before excitement

For each candidate, ask four questions. What is happening? Why should this viewer care? What context is required? Where does the thought or action finish?

A dramatic sentence can still be a poor candidate if it depends on ten minutes of background. A calm explanation can be a strong clip if it solves a specific problem quickly.

Listen to the sentence before and after your proposed boundaries. Watch for a “but,” “unless,” or correction that reverses the apparent point. Do not make a strong opening by cutting out the part that makes the statement accurate.

## Shape the opening around the real payoff

An opening should honestly prepare the viewer for what arrives. You can begin closer to the answer or demonstration, but the title and opening should not imply a different conclusion.

For example, a speaker's explanation of a failed recipe may work better starting with the failed loaf than with a long greeting. A gaming clip may need a moment establishing the challenge before the reaction becomes funny. The right opening depends on the content, not a universal number of seconds.

## Use transcripts and AI as a shortlist

A transcript helps locate phrases and compare possible sections. An AI tool may suggest candidates, but the suggestion is a starting point. The transcript can miss facial expressions, an on-screen action, sarcasm, and a joke that depends on timing.

Watch every proposed selection. Confirm the words, the context, and the actual ending. A “viral” score supplied by a product is not a guarantee of audience response.

If an automated selection begins halfway through a thought, repair the boundary before deciding whether the candidate is usable. If restoring context makes it unmanageably long, choose a different moment.

## Keep a record of what worked

After publishing, record the chosen idea, opening, length, and the available performance measures. Compare similar candidates over several posts. A result from a completely different topic may say more about the audience than the editing.

Use [the performance guide](/guides/measure-clip-performance/) to review the outcome. Over time, your candidate log becomes a record of editorial decisions you can learn from, instead of a collection of guesses.
