## Give the project a small scope

For your first project, aim to make one useful clip from one recording. Learning the entire editing application at once makes it difficult to tell whether a problem comes from the footage, the idea, or the software.

Choose a recording with clear speech or a visible action. Avoid a difficult audio repair job or a long multi-speaker panel until you understand trimming and reframing. Use footage you made, creator-supplied files, or footage you have permission to edit and publish.

Create a folder containing the source, an edit project, exports, and a short note with the source link and permission. Keep the original file unchanged. A simple filename such as `bread-demo-first-cut-v1.mp4` is easier to manage than several files all called “final.”

## Write the viewer's takeaway

Before opening the editor, write one sentence: “After watching, the viewer will understand…” or “The entertaining moment is…”. This sentence gives you a reason to keep or remove material.

For an illustrative cooking video, the takeaway might be that a dough was too wet and the creator shows how to recognize it. Keep the diagnosis and the demonstration. A long introduction about the creator's weekend probably does not help that particular clip.

If a section contains two unrelated takeaways, make two candidate clips rather than forcing both into one edit. See [how to find clippable moments](/guides/find-clippable-moments/) for a more detailed selection method.

## Make a plain cut first

Import the source into your editor, locate the selected moment, and split at its beginning and end. Remove the surrounding material from your working timeline. Keep enough setup to identify the subject and enough ending to finish the thought.

Watch the cut once without touching anything. Mark places that repeat the same point or leave the viewer waiting without a reason. Trim those places carefully. Pauses can communicate a reaction or let a point land, so do not delete silence automatically.

Now compare your edit with the surrounding original recording. Confirm that you have not removed a qualification, negation, or response that changes the speaker's meaning.

## Make the picture and speech readable

Set a canvas that suits the destination. For a portrait feed, a 9:16 layout is a common working choice. Move the source image inside that frame so the useful part remains visible. Check every change of speaker or subject.

Bring the voice to a comfortable level before adding music. If you use music, confirm you have the relevant rights and keep it below the speech. Test using ordinary phone speakers as well as headphones.

Generate captions if your editor supports them, then correct them against the recording. Names and numbers deserve special attention. Use short phrases and a stable placement that avoids interface controls. Our [caption guide](/guides/video-captions/) explains a readable style.

## Export, then check the actual file

Export a file rather than trusting the editor preview. Watch that file on a phone at a normal viewing size. Check the first frame, captions at the bottom, any crop changes, and the final sentence.

Ask someone unfamiliar with the full recording what the clip means. If they misunderstand the subject, restore the missing context before adding more effects. A clarity problem is rarely solved by a more elaborate transition.

Use the [publishing checklist](/tools/clip-checklist/) as your final pass. When you post, use an accurate title and link to the original source where relevant.

## Decide what to practice next

In a short [Primal Video lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1dd3zo7ELj4), Justin Brown recommends finishing an edit and improving through subsequent projects. A practical stopping rule is to check for concrete errors, resolve those, and save optional stylistic experiments for the next version.

Keep the first exported version. Write one thing that worked and one thing to improve. Your next project can focus on a single skill: cleaner captions, more deliberate opening cuts, or following speakers within a vertical frame.

Do not judge the entire skill by the view count of a first post. The immediate goal is a complete, understandable, correctly exported clip. Once that is reliable, use [performance data](/guides/measure-clip-performance/) to refine your choices.
