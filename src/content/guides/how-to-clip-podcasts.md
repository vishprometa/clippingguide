## Find an answer that stands on its own

Podcast episodes contain conversations, not ready-made short videos. Start by finding an answer, story, or exchange with a clear destination. Write down what the viewer should understand after watching it.

The best candidate for a practical clip may be a specific explanation rather than the episode's loudest moment. An anecdote also needs a beginning, development, and payoff. If the payoff depends on a long explanation elsewhere in the episode, it may be the wrong candidate for a short clip.

## Keep the question when it supplies context

An answer such as “three years” makes no sense without the question. Sometimes you can retain the spoken question. Sometimes a concise, accurate on-screen question supplies the same context without an extended setup.

Do not rewrite a question to make the guest appear to answer something different. Check whether the speaker is describing a personal example, a general recommendation, or a hypothetical. Those are different claims.

Review the exchange around your proposed boundaries. A response from the host or a later correction may be necessary to represent the conversation fairly.

## Plan the frame for each speaker

In a two-person wide shot, a simple center crop often cuts both people awkwardly. A portrait edit can alternate between speakers, keep a wider inset, or use a designed layout that fits the source footage.

Choose based on what the moment needs. A reaction from the listener can be part of the story, so following only the person speaking can remove useful information. Avoid constant switching during a calm explanation; the viewer needs time to settle on the speaker.

If the source has separate camera angles, use them to make the framing clearer. If it does not, avoid an extreme crop that makes the picture soft or hides important gestures.

## Make speech easy to hear and read

Check the guest and host separately. One microphone may be much quieter than the other. Balance the dialogue before adding any music. Listen for a sudden change at an edit boundary.

Use captions for meaningful speech and correct unfamiliar names, acronyms, and numbers. If the dialogue overlaps, avoid presenting a confident transcription when the words are unclear. Return to the source and listen carefully.

For translated clips, separate translation from interpretation. If you are unsure about a nuanced phrase, ask a competent speaker of the source language to check it before publishing. Humor, sarcasm, and professional terminology can be particularly easy to misrepresent.

## Package the clip honestly

Use a title that describes the idea or question answered. An impressive claim should not become broader in the title than it was in the conversation. Link to the full episode and, when practical, include a timestamp.

Creator permission and the episode's third-party assets still matter. A podcast intro song, sponsor clip, or inserted film excerpt can have rights beyond the guest's permission. Our [permissions guide](/guides/clipping-permissions/) covers the basic checks.

## Review a batch without making every clip identical

Batch editing can help with repeated tasks such as captions and export. It should not force every conversation into identical pacing. A demonstration, a serious explanation, and a quick joke each need different timing.

Keep a list of candidate topics and avoid posting several edits of the same answer as though they are separate lessons. Check the [publishing checklist](/tools/clip-checklist/) and track performance by topic as well as edit style.
